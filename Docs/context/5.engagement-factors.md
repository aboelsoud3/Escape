The games people remember years later almost always make them think:

"Just one more run."

That's the feeling you want to engineer.

The Psychology of "One More Try"

Most addictive games create a loop that lasts 30–90 seconds.

Challenge
↓
Player learns
↓
Small success
↓
Reward
↓
Slightly harder challenge
↓
Player believes:
"I can do even better."

That belief is more important than the reward itself.

The brain stays engaged because decisions are continuous.

2. Skill Ceiling

A beginner should survive:

20 seconds

An expert:

20 minutes

Without changing the controls.

Think about Tetris:

Same controls forever.
Infinite room to improve.
3. Fair Deaths

The player should think:

"That was my mistake."

Never:

"The game cheated."

That difference determines whether players restart immediately or quit.

4. Near Misses

This is one of the strongest psychological hooks.

Examples:

Jumping over a truck with only a tiny margin.
Sliding under a barrier at the last possible moment.
Barely squeezing between obstacles.

The closer the escape, the stronger the emotional response.

6. Mastery

The best games let players feel noticeably better after an hour.

Not because of stronger equipment—

Because they improved.

Examples:

Better timing.
Better anticipation.
Better route choices.
7. Tiny Rewards Every Few Seconds

The brain loves constant reinforcement.

Every 3–5 seconds:

Coin.
Combo.
Perfect dodge.
Speed boost.
New score milestone.

Even if each reward is small, together they keep players engaged.

8. "Juice"

A game with identical mechanics can feel completely different depending on feedback.

When you collect an item:

Instead of:

Collected

Use:

Small particle burst.
Screen shake.
Sound.
Flash.
Increasing combo sound.
Floating score.

All together, these make simple actions satisfying.

9. Randomness With Structure

Pure randomness feels unfair.

Instead:

Obstacle A

Obstacle B

Obstacle C

can appear in many different patterns, but every pattern remains solvable.

Players learn to adapt rather than memorize.

10. The "Almost PB" Effect

Suppose the player's best distance is:

1043 m

They lose at:

1027 m

Their first thought is often:

"I was so close."

That feeling is incredibly powerful. Good games surface personal-best progress so players can see when they're close.

If I wanted to win the contest...

I wouldn't try to make "Pepsiman."

I'd build a game with one unforgettable mechanic.

Examples:

Reality Shift

Every 20 seconds:

Gravity rotates.

The road becomes the wall.

The player keeps running.

Time Echo

You're chased by ghost versions of your previous runs.

If they catch you...

Game over.

Mirror World

The level is mirrored.

Left becomes right.

The player must adapt instantly.

Size Shift

The runner grows or shrinks.

Some obstacles require being tiny.

Others require being giant.

Chain Reaction

Destroying one obstacle changes the next 20.

The environment evolves based on your decisions.

The "Nintendo Test"

Nintendo often builds games around a single strong idea and then explores every interesting possibility it creates.

If your core mechanic can be summarized in one sentence—

"You run while gravity rotates around the world."

—and people immediately imagine new situations that could arise from it, you probably have a strong foundation.

My recipe for an award-worthy 1.44 MB entry

If I were optimizing for judges and players, I'd aim for this balance:

40% — A single original gameplay mechanic that people can explain in one sentence.
25% — Tight controls and fair, responsive movement.
15% — Excellent visual and audio feedback ("juice") despite simple graphics.
10% — Procedural variation that creates fresh but fair runs.
10% — Polish: smooth menus, satisfying sound, performance, and attention to detail.

A small game that does one thing exceptionally well is usually remembered longer than a larger game that tries to do many things adequately.

One last thought: if I were mentoring a team for this contest, I'd spend the first week building and testing only the core mechanic. If people aren't saying "one more run" before you've added fancy graphics, extra levels, or music, those additions won't fix the underlying design. The core loop should already be compelling in its simplest form.
