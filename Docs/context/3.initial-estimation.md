The real challenge isn't programming

The biggest challenge in a 1.44 MB contest is content creation without storing content.

Instead of including:

20 building models
50 textures
100 sound files

You generate them at runtime.

For example:

Road:

Generate spline
↓
Generate mesh
↓
Apply shader

Trees:

Random seed
↓
Recursive branches
↓
Leaves

Music:

Seed
↓
Synthesiser
↓
Music

Once you think procedurally, the size limit becomes much less restrictive.

Could it fit under 1.44 MB?

Yes.

A rough breakdown might look like:

Executable: 350–600 KB
Code: included in executable
Audio: 100–300 KB (procedural or tiny encoded clips)
Fonts: 5–20 KB
Miscellaneous data: 50–100 KB

Total:
700 KB–1.2 MB, leaving room for polish.

Many entries in size-constrained competitions go even smaller by generating nearly everything from mathematical descriptions.

What I'd choose

If I were entering this contest today, I'd use:

Language: C++
Library: raylib
Build: CMake
Compiler: Clang or GCC with -Os/LTO and stripped symbols
Assets: Mostly procedural
Audio: Procedural synthesis or very compact generated effects
Rendering: Simple low-poly 3D

That stack balances fast development with excellent control over binary size.

Considering your background

You're already comfortable with large software systems, algorithms, and Linux tooling. The main learning curve isn't C++; it's real-time game architecture and procedural content generation.

If you're willing to dedicate evenings and weekends, I think you could produce a contest-quality entry in about 6–10 weeks. If you've already built games before, that could shrink to 4–6 weeks.

One final suggestion: rather than recreating Pepsiman, take its core idea (fast forward motion and obstacle avoidance) and add one distinctive mechanic—such as physics-based movement, dynamic world transformations, or a risk/reward system. A memorable twist is often what separates a technically competent entry from one that judges remember.
